## Intro

### 1. Intro, paragraph 2
**BEFORE:** I don't read that as a mess. A venture firm, a research team, a consultancy, a network engineer, and a founder are answering different questions with the same word. None of them is wrong. So this chapter is a map first. The one-line claim the rest of the book hangs on, the spine, lives on the book page. What follows here is the research, then the definition I actually use, then a way to tell how far along a company is. The book is written in public. **AFTER (three paragraphs):**
I don't read that as a mess. A venture firm, a research team, a consultancy, a network engineer and a founder are each answering a different question, and they happen to reach for the same word to do it. None of them is wrong. They are looking at different parts of the same thing, which is why this chapter starts with a map of those meanings rather than a ruling on which one is correct.

The chapter moves in three steps. First comes the research: what nine sources mean by "AI-native" and what I take from each one. Then comes the definition I actually use, along with how it changed between July and November 2026. Last comes a practical test, seven questions you can ask a company to see how far along it is.

Like the rest of the book, this chapter is written in public, and I expect parts of it to change. When they do, the changelog at the bottom of the page records what changed and when.

**WHY:** One paragraph held four ideas. It also used an internal label ("the spine") that a reader can't decode.

## Why "AI-native" means several things at once

**AFTER:** Each of these sources was written for a purpose. A founder's guide helps founders, a spending report tracks where money goes, and a telecom white paper describes networks. ## The map

### 3. CRV, take
**BEFORE:** What it emphasizes is the product: whether the AI is the thing or a feature on the thing, and whether better models help you. I take both questions. They are a clear way to look at what a company sells, which is the job of a founder's guide, and I use them for that. How the company is built is the other side of the same subject, and that is what the test at the end of this page is for. Roles follow from that construction. They are a corollary of the definition, and I come back to them later.

**AFTER:** What CRV emphasizes is the product: whether the AI is the thing itself or a feature attached to it, and whether better models make the product stronger. I take both questions. A founder's guide is meant to help people judge what a company sells, and these two questions do that well. There is another side to the same subject, though: how the company itself is built, meaning who does the work, where its knowledge lives and who decides. That side is what the test at the end of this chapter is for. Questions about roles, such as which work people do and which agents do, follow from how the company is built, and I return to them in the chapters on judgment and organization.

**WHY:** "Roles follow from that construction. They are a corollary..." was two clipped sentences with no antecedent. The new version also says where roles come back.

### 4. IBM, take
**BEFORE:** What it emphasizes is the system: AI as a core component of the design, not a layer added at the end, and a path for companies that were not born this way. I take all three. IBM's definition is well aligned with the one I use. Who does which job follows from that construction, and I go further on it in the chapters on judgment and organization. I also take the prediction. The word will fade. The design question will not.

**AFTER:** What IBM emphasizes is the system. AI is part of the design from the start rather than a layer added at the end, and there is a path for companies that were not born this way. I take both points, and of all the sources here, IBM's definition is the closest to the one I use. I also take its prediction. As AI becomes ordinary, the word "AI-native" will probably stop meaning much, the way "internet company" did. The question it points at, how a company is designed around people and agents, will still be there after the label fades.

**WHY:** "I take all three" counted badly: the summary listed two things, and the third was the prediction. The roles sentence repeated CRV word for word. The paragraph ended on three fragments.

The second is the "queryable" company: a firm whose knowledge, decisions and history can be read and written as one system, so that what it learns flows straight back into what it does. **WHY:** "An open loop closed" was private shorthand. The new version explains it with the source's own image.

### 13. Third and fourth paragraphs
**BEFORE:** There is a second parallel, closer to the work itself. Building has gotten cheaper before. Over roughly the past fifteen years, standing up a store went from a custom project to something a person can do in a weekend: hosted commerce, payments, off-the-shelf logistics. The same thing happened. When the making gets cheap, the advantage moves, toward taste, distribution, and the operation around the tool. A cheap store did not make a good merchant.

Where it is not the same is the width of the wave. That one cheapened a layer. You could open the store without a team of engineers, and you still hired people to merchandise, to talk to customers, and to fulfill. This wave reaches those jobs too. Building, selling, support, and the loop that connects them can all move at once. Last time, cheaper building changed the store. This time it reaches the company around it.

**AFTER:**
There is a second parallel, closer to the work itself, because building has gotten cheaper before. Over roughly the past fifteen years, opening an online store went from a custom software project to something one person could set up in a weekend, thanks to hosted commerce platforms, online payments and off-the-shelf logistics. What happened next is instructive. Once the making got cheap, the advantage moved elsewhere: to taste, to distribution, and to everything that has to happen around the tool. A cheap store did not make anyone a good merchant.

The difference this time is how much of the company the change reaches. The last wave made one layer cheaper. You could open a store without a team of engineers, but you still hired people to choose the products, talk to customers and ship the orders. This wave reaches those jobs too. Building, selling, support and the loop that connects them can all change at the same time. Last time, cheaper building changed the store. This time it reaches the whole company around it.

**WHY:** "The same thing happened" had no referent. "The width of the wave" and "that one cheapened a layer" were compressed metaphors. The closing line is kept because the paragraph now earns it.

## My working definition, and how it evolved

### 19. Second paragraph
**BEFORE:** That's the part a small team can learn faster than a large one. It's the part a competitor can't download: the tool downloads, the context doesn't. And it's the part that's hardest to see from outside.

**AFTER:** That aspect matters for three reasons. A small team can learn it faster than a large one, because it has less to undo. A competitor can't simply copy it: anyone can download the same tools, but no one can download the knowledge a company has built up about its customers, its decisions and its own work. And it is the part that is hardest to see from outside, which is why the test later in this chapter has to ask from the inside.

**WHY:** The old version leaned on three "It's the part..." openers and a slogan. The new one explains each reason.

### 20. Cautions paragraph (removes the 40/60/80 line Lorenzo didn't follow)
**BEFORE:** Two cautions travel with it. It isn't a moat. It's the rate at which you build one, and the edge over incumbents is real but temporary. And it isn't binary. Companies are 40%, 60% or 80% of the way there, and the direction matters as much as the level.

**AFTER:** Two cautions come with this definition. The first is that being AI-native is not a moat in itself. It is the speed at which a company can build one, and the advantage it gives over incumbents is real but temporary, because incumbents can rebuild too. The second is that it isn't a yes-or-no label. Companies sit at different points along the way, and the direction a company is moving in matters as much as where it stands today.

**WHY:** The fragments are gone. The percentages were the passage Lorenzo said he didn't understand, and they implied a precision we don't have.
