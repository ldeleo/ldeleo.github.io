# Book style guide: *The AI-Native Company*

Status: v1, Nov 16, 2026. Written after Lorenzo's feedback on Nov 16, 2026 (t88u): the book read as "so efficient that it is hard to read... robotic... You are writing for humans, not machines. This is not a social media post, it's a book. It's ok to explain more."

**Scope.** This guide covers the web book only: the landing page, every topic page, captions, callouts and diagram text. **The social-post rules do not apply here.** Those rules are short sentences, one point per line, cutting every extra word and ending on a punch. They work for LinkedIn and X. In a book they produce the choppy, telegraphic prose Lorenzo flagged. If a rule here conflicts with a post rule, this guide wins on the book.

The content rules still apply everywhere. He is an investor and a visionary, never a consultant. Never state or imply he is actively investing. He is the only human in his AI-native setup, so no misleading "we". Nothing arrogant. Most startups he meets are not AI-native yet. Other sources are research he builds on, not foils. No LP, deal, family or fee figures. Never call his system a CRM.

---

## 1. Who we are writing for

Write for a smart reader who was not in our conversations. That might be a founder, an operator or a potential LP reading on a Sunday. They have not seen the diagram's design brief, they do not know what "the spine" means internally, and they cannot ask what we meant.

Pinker calls the main failure here the **curse of knowledge**: the writer cannot imagine not knowing what they know, so they skip steps, compress ideas into private shorthand and leave terms undefined ([Pinker, *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3, summary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-sense-of-style/chapters-3-4-summary/); [IEEE Micro review](https://doi.org/10.1109/mm.2015.67)). Most of our "efficient" sentences are this curse. They are clear to us because we already know the argument.

**Do:** the first time a key term appears (construction, shared memory, loop, judgment point, context), explain it in plain words or give a concrete example.
**Don't:** use internal labels such as "the spine", "the build" or "the construction" as if the reader already owns them.

## 2. One idea per paragraph

A paragraph is a promise that its sentences belong together. If two unrelated thoughts share a paragraph, the reader looks for a link that isn't there. Williams: a coherent paragraph has consistent topic strings and usually one sentence that states its point, near the start or at the end ([Williams, *Style*, "Coherence"](https://marcuse.faculty.history.ucsb.edu/classes/204writingworkshop/pdfs/Williams1995CoherenceConcisionOCR.pdf)).

**Test:** can you name the paragraph's single point in a few words? If you need "and" to name it, split the paragraph.

Before (landing):
> That sentence is the spine. Every topic hangs off it. The book is written in public, which means a view can change. When it does, the change is recorded on the page, with a date, the way a definition gets a version rather than a silent edit.

There are two ideas here: how the definition organizes the book, and how the book handles changes. That makes two paragraphs. See the landing rewrite.

## 3. Every "this", "that" and "it" must point to something visible

A pronoun or a "that sentence" has to refer to something the reader saw a moment ago. Across a diagram, a heading or a callout, the referent is lost.

**Do:** repeat the noun ("the definition above", "this diagram", "that split between tools that help people and tools that replace a task").
**Don't:** open a paragraph with "That sentence", "This", "It" or "The same thing happened" when anything sits between it and its referent.

## 4. Old information first, new information last

Begin each sentence with something the reader already has, and end with what is new. The end of one sentence then becomes the start of the next. This is what makes prose flow instead of reading like a list ([Williams, old-to-new](https://english109mercy.wordpress.com/2017/11/13/joseph-williams-explains-old-to-new/)). Use transitions that name the logical relation: because, so, but, for example, the difference is, that is why. Pinker's "arcs of coherence" are these connections: resemblance, sequence, cause and effect ([Pinker, ch. 5, summary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-sense-of-style/chapters-5-6-summary/)). Zinsser: transitions "must pull the reader from one sentence to the next without noticing the tug," and the last sentence of each paragraph is "the crucial springboard to the next" ([Zinsser, *On Writing Well*](https://archive.org/stream/OnWritingWell/on-writing-well_djvu.txt); [notes](https://thekeypoint.org/2022/09/11/on-writing-well/)).

## 5. Rhythm: vary sentence length and avoid runs of fragments

Gary Provost's demonstration shows the problem: "This sentence has five words. Here are five more words... The writing is getting boring" ([Provost, *100 Ways to Improve Your Writing*, via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Provost)). Our pages read like the first half of that passage.

**Rules of thumb:**
- Never write three sentences in a row under about eight words.
- Allow at most one deliberate fragment per section, and only for emphasis after a longer sentence has earned it.
- Mix short, medium and long sentences. Long sentences are fine when their structure is simple: subject, verb, then the rest.
- No telegraphic openers ("Six topics." "Two cautions." "Three cautions."). Write a sentence: "The book is organized in six topics."

## 6. Explain, then summarize, and use examples

Explaining is the job of a book. Give the idea, then the reason, then an example or a short historical comparison, and only then the summary line or table. Morgan Housel's essays carry abstract ideas through concrete stories ([Housel, "Best Story Wins"](https://collabfund.com/blog/story/)). Our best paragraphs already do this: the cloud-native history and the online-store comparison.

Callouts, tables and diagram captions **summarize** what the prose has already explained. They never replace it. A slogan such as "The tool downloads. The context doesn't." can close a paragraph that has explained it, but it can't stand in for one.

## 7. Write like he talks, in the first person

Paul Graham's test: read each sentence and ask, "Is this the way I'd say this if I were talking to a friend?" ([Graham, "Write Like You Talk"](https://www.paulgraham.com/talk.html)). Graham also says an essay is an attempt to figure something out, not a brief for a position ([Graham, "The Age of the Essay"](https://www.paulgraham.com/essay.html)). That fits a book written in public. Zinsser asks writers to keep the human "I" and the warmth ([Zinsser](https://thekeypoint.org/2022/09/11/on-writing-well/)).

**Do:** use "I" for Lorenzo's views and experience, and "you" for the reader when it helps. Say "I think" or "from what I see" where it's an opinion.
**Don't:** use a "we" that suggests a team. Don't use stiff abstractions ("The honest way in is a map"), and don't use marketing cadence.

## 8. Orient the reader: signposts at the top of every page

Keep what already works: key points up top, a contents list and clear section headings. Add a short "how this chapter moves" paragraph in prose ("First... then... finally..."). Pinker reports that readers understand and remember more when the topic and point are stated early ([Pinker, ch. 5](https://www.supersummary.com/the-sense-of-style/chapters-5-6-summary/)). Signposting is not padding. It is the map the reader needs to keep going.

## 9. Length: clarity first, then completeness, then brevity

Cut repetition and anything that doesn't work, but never cut the explanation. Graham's "cut ruthlessly" applies to what bores the reader, not to the steps a reader needs ([Graham, "Writing, Briefly"](https://www.paulgraham.com/writing44.html)). If a sentence only makes sense to someone who knows the argument, it is not concise. It is incomplete.

Also cut **repetition of a stock phrase across a page**. "A map, not a verdict" appeared three times on the Definition page. Say it well once.

## Before and after, from his pages

**Unclear referent plus two ideas in one paragraph (landing)**
Before: "That sentence is the spine. Every topic hangs off it. The book is written in public, which means a view can change. When it does, the change is recorded on the page, with a date, the way a definition gets a version rather than a silent edit."
After: "The diagram is that definition drawn out. On one side are the people and on the other the agents, and between them sits a single memory that both can read and write. The work moves around the loop, and the marked points are the moments where a person, not a model, makes the decision. Each topic in this book takes one part of this picture and looks at it closely."
New paragraph, under its own heading: "This book is being written in public, one topic at a time, and that has a consequence worth stating at the start: some of my views will change as I learn. When one does, I won't quietly rewrite the page..."

**Mechanical fragments (landing)**
Before: "Six topics. Definition is the one you can read now. The others are coming, and the book grows in public as they do."
After: "The book is organized in six topics. The first, Definition, is ready to read now. It looks at what different people mean by 'AI-native', sets out the definition this book uses, and offers seven questions for judging how far along a company is. The other five are in progress, and each will appear here when it is published."

**"The same thing happened" with no referent (Definition)**
Before: "Over roughly the past fifteen years, standing up a store went from a custom project to something a person can do in a weekend: hosted commerce, payments, off-the-shelf logistics. The same thing happened. When the making gets cheap, the advantage moves..."
After: "Over roughly the past fifteen years, opening an online store went from a custom software project to something one person could set up in a weekend, thanks to hosted commerce platforms, online payments and off-the-shelf logistics. What happened next is instructive. Once the making got cheap, the advantage moved elsewhere..."

**Compressed private shorthand (Definition, Y Combinator)**
Before: "The second is the queryable company, the firm that can be read and written as one system, an open loop closed."
After: "The second is the 'queryable' company: a firm whose knowledge, decisions and history can be read and written as one system, so that what it learns flows straight back into what it does. That is what YC means by turning an open loop into a closed one."

## Self-review checklist (run before anything goes to Lorenzo)

1. Read the page aloud. Mark every spot where you stumble or sound like a press release.
2. For each paragraph, name its one point. If you need "and", split it.
3. Circle every "this / that / it / the same". Is the referent in the previous sentence or two, with nothing in between?
4. Look for three or more short sentences in a row, and for fragments that open paragraphs. Rewrite them.
5. At its first use, does every key term have a plain explanation or an example?
6. Does every table, callout and caption follow prose that already explained the idea?
7. Is any stock phrase repeated across the page?
8. Run the content guards: no consultant framing, no active-investing implication, no misleading "we", nothing arrogant, no LP, deal, family or fee figures.
9. Read it as a demanding outside editor, improve it, and send it with a short note on what changed.
